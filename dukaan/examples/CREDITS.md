mine, so they are listed here with their source and licence.

All four are released under CC0 1.0 (public domain dedication), which is why
they were chosen: the creatives derived from them carry no further conditions.

They stand in for what a small seller would actually shoot: one object, one
plain surface, one phone. Museum object photography has the same shape as a
good counter photo, and unlike stock product shots it is unambiguously free.

## A limitation worth stating

The cutout separates product from backdrop by colour, so it needs the two to
differ. A pale product on a pale sweep defeats it: a white porcelain teapot on a
light grey backdrop matches the fitted surface across its own body and comes out
hollow, keeping only its darker rim and decoration.

Connectivity was tried as a fix, on the theory that real backdrop reaches the
frame edge while the inside of a product does not. It does not work here. The
teapot's body touches the backdrop with no contrast boundary to stop a flood
fill, so the fill leaks straight through it, and worse, it breaks ring-shaped
products: the hole in the middle of a bangle is enclosed background that must
stay transparent, and connectivity fills it in. The experiment was reverted.

The honest answer is a matting model, which is the obvious upgrade and the one
place a second model would earn its VRAM. Until then: shoot the product against
something it contrasts with.
